01 — Clinical Context

Why This Matters

Clinical Problem

Knee osteoarthritis is a major musculoskeletal disease burden, particularly among older adults. Early structural changes can be difficult to identify consistently across routine clinical workflows.

Current Gaps

Imaging data are frequently siloed across X-ray, MRI, ultrasound, and CT systems. Longitudinal comparison and multimodal interpretation can therefore remain fragmented.

Proposed Solution

OmniSkel-AI is designed as a unified platform that can fuse multiple imaging modalities to detect abnormalities, quantify structural changes, track progression, and present interpretable findings.

Core principle: OmniSkel-AI should function as clinical decision support, keeping the clinician in the loop while making multimodal imaging analysis more structured, measurable, and explainable.

04 — Data Layer

Medical Imaging Data Pipeline

The data layer is responsible for ingestion, de-identification, normalization, quality control, preprocessing, annotation management, and dataset provenance.

Potential Data Sources

X-ray

AP and lateral knee radiographs from appropriately licensed research datasets and participating clinical institutions.

MRI

Knee MRI sequences such as proton-density fat-saturated, T1-weighted, and T2-weighted acquisitions.

Ground Truth

Radiologist annotations, structural measurements, osteoarthritis grades, cartilage annotations, and meniscal pathology labels where available.

Preprocessing Pipeline

1

DICOM

Validate and standardize imaging metadata and pixel data.

2

De-identify

Remove or transform protected health information before research processing.

3

Normalize

Harmonize spatial resolution, orientation, intensity, and scanner-related variation.

4

Quality Control

Detect incomplete, corrupted, misaligned, or unsuitable studies.

Processing Techniques

  • DICOM standardization and conversion to research-friendly volumetric representations such as NIfTI.
  • Anatomical landmark alignment and spatial normalization.
  • MRI bias-field correction using methods such as N4.
  • Histogram and scanner harmonization where scientifically justified.
  • Data augmentation for class imbalance and robustness.
  • Synthetic-data generation using diffusion or other generative methods only after rigorous validation.

Data integrity rule: Synthetic images should never silently enter the validation or test set. Real-world held-out data must remain isolated for meaningful performance evaluation.

05 — AI Model Layer

MultiSkelFormer

The proposed Phase 1 core architecture is MultiSkelFormer, a modular multimodal transformer designed to align information from 2D X-ray imagery and 3D MRI volumes.

Input

X-ray Pathway

Two-dimensional radiographs processed through a dedicated visual encoder with spatial normalization.

MRI Pathway

Three-dimensional MRI volumes processed through a volumetric encoder capable of learning spatial and anatomical features.

Fusion

Cross-modal attention allows information from one modality to condition interpretation of the other. For example, X-ray structural information can provide contextual information for MRI-derived soft-tissue features.

Model Heads

Detection Head

Detect abnormal regions such as osteophytes and other predefined pathology targets.

Segmentation Head

Segment cartilage, menisci, bone, and other anatomical structures using architectures such as nnU-Net.

Measurement Head

Estimate quantitative measurements such as joint-space width, femoral alignment, and tibial slope.

Longitudinal Head

Model change across repeated examinations using temporal architectures and attention.

Candidate Technologies

  • PyTorch
  • MONAI
  • nnU-Net
  • 3D ResNet / 3D Vision Transformer
  • EfficientNet-V2
  • Attention mechanisms
  • Temporal CNN / LSTM
06 — Explainability

Explainable AI

Explainability is treated as a first-class component of the platform rather than a post-processing feature.

Grad-CAM / Grad-CAM++

Generate visual attribution maps showing image regions that contributed to a model prediction.

SHAP

Analyze feature contributions for appropriate structured measurements and prediction models.

Structured Findings

Convert model outputs into machine-readable findings before any natural-language generation occurs.

Human Review

Allow radiologists to inspect, accept, reject, or correct individual AI findings.

07 — Reporting

Clinical Report Generation

The reporting system should transform validated structured findings into readable clinical language while preserving traceability to the underlying model outputs.

Recommended architecture: clinical measurements and validated findings are generated first; the natural-language layer only verbalizes those structured outputs. It should not independently invent diagnoses, measurements, or treatment recommendations.

Example Report

IMPRESSION
Moderate radiographic osteoarthritis of the right knee.
• Medial joint-space width is reduced compared with the
  available baseline examination.
• MRI demonstrates a suspected posterior-horn meniscal tear.
• Quantitative measurements and confidence values are
  available in the AI analysis panel.
Radiologist review required before clinical use.
08 — Clinical Workflow

Clinical Integration

EHR Integration

Use standards-based interfaces such as FHIR to retrieve authorized clinical context where appropriate.

PACS Integration

Integrate with imaging workflows through DICOM-compatible infrastructure and viewers such as OHIF.

Radiologist-in-the-Loop

Present AI findings for review, correction, acceptance, or rejection before they become part of the clinical record.

Workflow

1. Imaging Study Received DICOM study enters the AI processing environment.
↓
2. Automated Quality Control Validate modality, anatomy, orientation, completeness, and technical quality.
↓
3. AI Analysis Detection, segmentation, measurement, and multimodal fusion.
↓
4. Explainability Generate attribution maps and structured evidence.
↓
5. Radiologist Review Accept, reject, modify, or annotate AI findings.
↓
6. Final Clinical Report Human-reviewed findings are incorporated into the clinical workflow.

10 — Security & Compliance

Security Architecture

Security, privacy, governance, and clinical validation should be designed into the platform from the beginning rather than added after model development.

AES-256

Encryption at rest.

TLS 1.3

Encrypted data transmission.

RBAC

Role-based access control and least-privilege access.

Audit

Immutable or tamper-evident access and workflow logs.

PHI

Protected health information controls and de-identification.

Consent

Data-use and consent-management workflows.

Regulatory Strategy

A future medical-device pathway should be developed with regulatory specialists and based on the final intended use, claims, risk classification, software functions, clinical evidence, and applicable jurisdiction.

Important: Regulatory classification and clearance pathways should be treated as project workstreams to be validated with the relevant regulatory authority. The architecture should not assume that a specific clearance route is guaranteed.

11 — Validation

Phase 1 Validation Framework

The prototype should establish predefined performance targets and evaluate them on patient-level held-out data. Any previously reported performance numbers must be traceable to their underlying dataset, protocol, and publication or experiment.

Task Candidate Metric Phase 1 Target Validation Requirement
OA detection AUC / sensitivity / specificity > 0.90 AUC target Patient-level held-out test set
Meniscal tear detection F1 / sensitivity / specificity > 0.85 F1 target Independent MRI evaluation
JSW measurement MAE / agreement < 0.2 mm target Reader comparison
Longitudinal change Measurement error Predefined before testing Repeated-study cohort
Calibration Calibration curve / Brier score Predefined Independent evaluation

Validation Principles

  • Split data at the patient level.
  • Prevent patient leakage between train and test sets.
  • Keep the final test set locked until model development ends.
  • Report confidence intervals.
  • Measure calibration, not only discrimination.
  • Evaluate performance across relevant demographic and technical subgroups.
  • Perform external validation on data from a different institution or acquisition environment.
  • Compare AI outputs with appropriately defined clinical reference standards.

Evidence discipline: claims such as "94% agreement," "35% time saved," or specific hospital pilot results should only appear in the production specification after the underlying evidence has been verified.

15 — Implementation Roadmap

Repository setup, data governance, DICOM pipeline, dataset acquisition, preprocessing framework, experiment tracking, and baseline models.

Implement X-ray and MRI encoders, segmentation baseline, pathology detection, and measurement models.

Implement MultiSkelFormer fusion, cross-modal attention, multimodal representation learning, and ablation studies.

Add saliency maps, structured findings, confidence estimation, measurement explanations, and clinician-facing visualization.

Integrate DICOM/PACS infrastructure, OHIF-based visualization, FHIR interfaces, radiologist review, audit logging, and report generation.

Lock the evaluation dataset, execute validation experiments, quantify performance and uncertainty, document limitations, and prepare the next-stage clinical research plan.

17 — Engineering Principles

Design Principles

Security Before Features

Patient data protection and access control are foundational system requirements.

Tests Before Deployment

Every production model and software release should pass automated and domain-specific validation.

Evidence Before Claims

Performance claims must be reproducible and traceable to documented experiments or external evidence.

Human Oversight

Clinical decisions remain under qualified human supervision.

Modular by Design

New modalities, anatomical regions, and models should be independently extensible.

Explainability by Design

The platform should expose evidence and uncertainty alongside predictions.

18 — Vision

Knee First. Entire Skeleton Next.

OmniSkel-AI begins with a focused knee-imaging prototype and is architected from the beginning for expansion into a broader musculoskeletal intelligence platform.

The long-term architecture combines multimodal medical imaging, anatomical representation learning, quantitative biomarkers, longitudinal reasoning, explainability, clinical workflow integration, and human-in-the-loop validation.

Vision: Build an extensible intelligence layer for musculoskeletal imaging that helps clinicians understand not only what is visible, but also how structures change over time and which measurable findings support each AI output.
